# Aevum: Age of Shrines — Lore

## The World Before

<!-- Source: assets/md/prologue.md — edit the origin story there, not here -->

Three hundred years ago, dragons ruled the sky over Aevum, and the ground belonged to no one. There were no clans, no borders — just people, hunted, generation after generation, until the dragons pushed hard enough that survival finally demanded one last stand. The final war was cataclysmic: new weapons were forged in it, desperate spells discovered in the worst hours of it, and when it ended, the dragons were gone — or so everyone believed. The cost was total. Entire regions were left scorched, flooded, hollowed out, and the people who'd won were left scattered across a world that no longer resembled the one they remembered.

For generations after, survival was the only concern. There was no shared purpose beyond staying alive in a broken world. Slowly — over decades, then centuries — survivors found each other again and gathered into like-minded groups. It was in that long process of regrouping, not in the war itself, that specialization emerged: smiths becoming weaponmakers, healers becoming battlefield clerics, hunters becoming those who could read terrain no one else could. What began as scattered instinct hardened, generation by generation, into the founding disciplines of the clans Aevum knows today.

Those clans now hold ground their ancestors bled for, increasingly territorial over the shrines that memorialize a war none of them lived through. Alliances form and break over old grudges dressed up as new ones. And now word is spreading: two dragons never died. They went silent instead. They've been guarding something.

The last egg of their kind is about to hatch — or it's about to be destroyed forever. Every clan in Aevum has already decided which outcome they can live with. Not all of them agree.

## The Shrines

The shrines predate the clans. They were built during the war by people who expected to die and wanted to leave something behind — not monuments to victory, but markers of the cost. Each one carries a mantra: a short phrase distilled from whatever that place witnessed. Meditating at a shrine is not worship. It is remembering. It is standing still long enough to understand what was sacrificed here, and why.

The clans have since claimed the shrines as their own, insisting that each one belongs by nature to a particular discipline — that the Shrine of Truth was always the Mage's, that the Shrine of Valor was always the Fighter's. This is politics. The shrines belong to no one. But the meditation bonuses they grant are real, and they are clan-specific, which is close enough to ownership for practical purposes.

## The Dragon Egg

No one knows how long the egg has been there. The two surviving dragons — the Guardian and the Hunter — have circled it in silence for as long as anyone can remember, never attacking, never leaving. The egg is somewhere near the world's center. Anyone who has gotten close enough to confirm this has not come back to say where, exactly.

The clans disagree on what should happen to it:

- Some believe the egg must be destroyed. One war was survived. A second would not be.
- Some believe the egg must be secured — brought home, held, controlled. If a dragon is coming regardless, better it answers to someone.
- Some have not decided and are watching the others, waiting to see which argument wins before committing.

The game begins at the moment the egg becomes reachable. The dragons are stirring. The shrines are contested. The clans have made their choice.

### Why the egg, and not the dragons

Here is the part the clans understand and the histories rarely say plainly: nobody is trying to kill the Guardian or the Hunter. Nobody could. Two dragons who survived the war that ended all the others did not survive it by being the weak ones. What can be ended is not them — it is what comes after them.

For three hundred years, Guardian and Hunter have done exactly one thing: wait. Not because they are peaceful. Because they learned, at catastrophic cost, that a war against the clans is a war dragons lose in the end, however long it takes and however many towns burn getting there. So they chose the only strategy left to a species down to its final future: stay hidden, stay still, and let one clutch hatch somewhere the clans stopped looking. Guard the egg long enough, and dragonkind does not need to win a war. It only needs to survive long enough to become someone else's problem, generations from now.

That is what a shrine fire actually ends. Not two dragons — they will still be exactly as old, as scarred, and as dangerous the day after as the day before. What ends is the *waiting having a point*. An egg that will never hatch turns three hundred years of patience into three hundred years spent guarding nothing. The clans do not need to win the fight the last war proved unwinnable. They only need to make sure it was the last egg, permanently, and let time do what neither side could do to the other by force.

Some clans understand this and pursue the egg for exactly that reason. Others still believe a captured egg is a bargaining chip, a weapon, a future ally — they are not entirely wrong that *holding* it changes the balance of power while it exists, only wrong about what happens once a shrine's fire takes it. And some clans, in the deepest and worst-attended pub theories, wonder whether killing both dragons outright wouldn't be cleaner. History does not favor them. Two ancient predators, cornered with nothing left to protect, are not less dangerous. They are the war the first shrine mantras were written to remember — reopened, this time on purpose.

### How the war ends

Every game of Aevum ends one of three ways, and each one is a different answer to the same question: what happens to the last dragons?

**The egg is found and destroyed at a shrine — the clean ending.** A clan reaches the egg before it hatches and brings it home to burn. Guardian and Hunter are untouched, exactly as alive and exactly as dangerous as they always were — this was never a war anyone won by force. What ends is the future. Three hundred years of patient waiting turns out to have been for nothing, permanently. Fewest lives lost. Fewest towns burned. The quiet ending, and the one every clan that understands the stakes is racing toward.

**The dragons wake, and the clans do not reach the egg in time — the cataclysm repeats.** Every shrine mantra exists because the first war against the dragons nearly ended everyone. When the egg hatches unopposed, or the clans provoke Guardian and Hunter into the open before they're ready, the war the mantras were written to remember stops being history and starts being tomorrow. If every clan enclave falls before a single egg reaches a single shrine, the dragons have simply out-lasted the people who were supposed to stop them — the second war the first one was fought to prevent.

**The egg hatches, and the clans finish it the hard way.** Sometimes the clean ending is missed — the egg hatches before anyone reaches it, or a clan gambles on brute force over subtlety — and now there is a hatchling loose in the world alongside two adults who have spent three centuries proving they don't lose wars of attrition. There is no clean way to end this branch. It ends only when Guardian, Hunter, and every dragon that hatched are all dead — a war fought and won the costly way, the way the first war was, because the chance to avoid it is already gone.

## The Clans

The twelve clans of Aevum did not exist before the war. They formed in the centuries after it, out of the same impulse that produces all specialization: when survival is collective, whoever is best at something gets to keep doing it. The clans are not tribes or nations. They are disciplines that became communities that became territories that became identities.

**Fighter** — weapons and walls. The war produced them. They have not stopped since.

**Mage** — information and control. They discovered three of the war's most decisive spells. They have been insufferable about it.

**Cleric** — healing and endurance. They kept more people alive than any other group during the reconstruction. They remember this clearly.

**Dwarf** — construction and durability. The mountains were their refuge. They never came down all the way.

**Ranger** — movement and terrain. They were the scouts in the final war. They still move like it.

**Elf** — precision and distance. Long sight, longer memory. They were there before the war and they will be careful about what they do next.

**Rogue** — shadow and information. Technically independent. Technically.

**Monk** — discipline and stillness. They do not start fights. They have opinions about how fights end.

**Druid** — land and growth. They have been trying to fix what the war burned since the day it ended. They are not finished.

**Necromancer** — the dead and what they leave behind. The war left a great deal behind. Someone had to learn to work with it.

**Bard** — words and rumour. They remember the war the way it should be remembered, which is not necessarily the way it happened.

**Shaman** — storm and spirit. They talk to things the other clans pretend are not there.

## A Note on the Mantras

Each shrine carries one mantra from a pool of one hundred phrases, drawn fresh for each game. No two games share the same eight. A mantra is not a password or a prayer — it is a resonance. To meditate at a shrine, a unit must know the mantra beforehand, or be willing to sit in the silence long enough to find it. A direct teaching takes one turn. A rumoured phrase, half-remembered from a pub conversation, takes longer. An unknown mantra takes the longest of all.

The mantras themselves are not recorded here. They are spoken at the shrines, passed between characters in towns and taverns, and discovered in the ruins of the war. They change each game. That is intentional.
